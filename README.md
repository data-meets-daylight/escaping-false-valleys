**Written, illustrated and presented by Saf Flatters**  
Bachelor of Science (Data Science)  
Perth, Western Australia

### Statistical fiction about optimisation, randomness and finding your way through uncertainty.

In 2026, I entered the Royal Statistical Society and *Significance* Magazine's
Statistical Excellence Award for Early Career Writing with a slightly unusual idea:

**Could I use fiction to make someone curious about statistics?**

The result was *Escaping False Valleys* — a short story about a mountain-bike
rider lost in a mountain range, trying to find a town in the lowest valley.

Along the way, the story explores three ideas from statistics and data science:

- Global optimisation
- Stochasticity (randomness)
- Bayesian reasoning

I called this genre of writing **Statistical Fiction - or Stat-fi.**

## The competition

*Escaping False Valleys* was selected as one of three finalists in the
2026 Statistical Excellence Award for Early Career Writing. That gave me the incredible opportunity to travel from Perth, Western Australia
to the UK in September 2026 and present the work at the Royal Statistical
Society International Conference. Rather than simply explain the statistics behind the article, my presentation
was about why I chose to teach statistics through a story and how writing it
forced me to understand the concepts well enough to explain them to someone else.

I also drew and painted the statistical diagrams and all of the artwork used
throughout the story and presentation is by hand.

## About the idea

I've spent much of my career as an electrician troubleshooting electrical and
mechanical faults before moving into data science. One of the things that fascinated me when I started learning statistics was how
familiar some of the thinking felt. Fault finding often means starting with what you think is most likely, gathering
new evidence, updating your belief, and deciding what to test next. That connection eventually found its way into *Escaping False Valleys*.

The story uses a mountain-bike rider navigating an incomplete landscape to explore
what it means to make decisions when you can't see the whole problem:

*"The mountain no longer felt like her nemesis, just an incomplete dataset.  
The best route down was not the steepest one beneath her feet. It was the one she could only find by first escaping the local geometry.  
The mountain had not required heroism. It had required statistical thinking...     
the willingness to stop treating the nearest, easiest answer as the best one.   
To accept uncertainty.  
To search through a landscape she could not fully see.  
To update beliefs.  
And to allow a little randomness into the search."*
